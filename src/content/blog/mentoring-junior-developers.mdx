I started mentoring junior developers because the team needed it. I kept doing it because it made *me* a better engineer. If you've ever wondered whether mentoring is "worth it," this is my honest answer: it's one of the highest-leverage things you can do for your own growth.

## Explaining forces clarity

You cannot hand-wave through a concept in front of a junior dev the way you can inside your own head. When someone asks *"but why do we put this here?"* and you have to answer in plain words, you quickly find out which of your habits are deliberate and which are cargo cult.

I now apply the same standard to my own code: if I can't explain *why* in a sentence, the code needs work.

## Small review rituals that scale

The most effective habit we built was not reviewing code faster — it was reviewing it **together**, out loud. A ten-minute walkthrough catches more than an hour of silent review comments, because the author explains intent while the reviewer checks the behavior matches.

### A checklist I keep in mind when reviewing

- Does the name say what the thing *does*, not what it *is*?
- Is the state change obvious from the call site?
- Would this survive a year of feature additions?
- What did the author have to know to write this — and is that knowledge discoverable in the code?

## The confidence part is real

Most juniors don't lack ability. They lack the confidence to ask. Removing the fear of asking is often the single biggest productivity lever on a small team. That starts with how you respond to questions: thank people for asking, never shame a mistake twice, and make it safe to say "I don't know."

## Give it a try

You don't need a formal program. Mentor a summer intern, walk a new hire through their first pull request, or just write a blog post explaining something you know cold.

The person who learns the most is always the teacher.
