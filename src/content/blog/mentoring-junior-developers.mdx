Mentoring is one of the most rewarding things an engineer can do, and one of the hardest to do well. The goal isn't to produce code faster — it's to grow a person who can make good decisions long after you're gone.

## Teach the Reasoning, Not Just the Answer

When a junior brings you a question, resist the urge to hand over the fix. Walk through how you'd approach the problem: what you'd read, what you'd check, what trade-offs you'd weigh. The artifact they take away isn't the code — it's the thought process.

## Give Feedback on the Small Stuff Too

Big design reviews matter, but daily habits matter more. Naming, commit messages, and how they ask for help compound over time. Praise what's good so they know to repeat it, and be specific about what to change and why.

## Protect Their Autonomy

It's tempting to take over when something is slow or risky. Don't. Give them a clear outcome and let them choose the path. They'll make mistakes — that's the point. Your job is to keep those mistakes cheap and survivable.

## Make Yourself Replaceable

A good mentor works toward their own obsolescence. The success metric isn't how much they depend on you, but how well they function when you're not there.

## Be Patient, and Be Human

Growth is non-linear. Someone who seems stuck for weeks can leap forward overnight. Keep showing up, keep being honest, and remember that everyone you admire once had someone who didn't give up on them.
